# The Quiet Outsourcing of the Mind

A few weeks ago, I watched a friend try to write a two-line email to her landlord. She opened ChatGPT, typed a rough version of what she wanted to say, and waited for it to hand back a "better" one. It was a two-line email. She has a graduate degree. She has written thousands of emails in her life. But in that moment, she didn't trust herself to write two sentences without help.

I don't think she's lazy. I think she's early evidence of something bigger than any of us have fully reckoned with: we are not just using AI as a tool. We are handing it pieces of our minds, one small surrender at a time, and most of us haven't noticed the pattern yet.

## We've done this before — sort of

Socrates hated writing. In the *Phaedrus*, he warns that if people learn to write, they'll stop exercising their memory, and wisdom will curdle into the mere appearance of wisdom — people who've read things but never wrestled with them. He was, in a narrow sense, right: nobody memorizes epic poems anymore. And he was, in the larger sense, wrong: writing didn't destroy human thought, it *extended* it. It let us build law, science, and philosophy on top of a stable record instead of a fallible one.

Calculators did the same thing to arithmetic. GPS did it to spatial memory. Search engines did it to trivia. Each time, we lost a capacity and gained bandwidth for something else. This is the comfortable story we tell ourselves about AI, too: it's just the next tool in a long line of tools, and we'll adapt like we always have.

I think that story is missing something. Every offloading device before this one handled *execution*. A calculator doesn't decide what problem to solve — you do. A map doesn't decide where you should go — you do. AI is the first tool in human history that can perform the step before execution: it can *decide*. And that changes what's actually at stake.

## Three tiers of surrender

Not all offloading costs the same. It helps to separate what we're giving up into three tiers, because they escalate.

**Memory** is the oldest and least alarming tier. We stopped memorizing phone numbers, routes, facts we could look up in seconds. Something is lost here — there's real evidence that active recall strengthens the brain in ways that passive lookup doesn't — but the loss is modest and the trade has mostly been worth it.

**Thinking** is murkier. This is the drafting, outlining, working-through-a-problem-on-paper labor that used to be inseparable from having an idea in the first place. Increasingly, people skip the struggle: they ask AI for the first draft and edit from there. The problem is that in writing, the "struggle" isn't a regrettable side effect of thinking — it *is* the thinking. You don't know what you believe about something until you've tried, and failed, to say it clearly. Skip that step enough times and you don't get faster thinkers. You get people who are fluent in other people's conclusions.

**Judgment** is the tier that should actually worry us. This is the outsourcing of *deciding* — what's true, what's ethical, what's the right call in an ambiguous situation. Ask AI whether to take a job, how to interpret a friend's text message, whether a piece of news is credible, how to feel about a moral dilemma. We're not just skipping a boring step here. We're skipping the part of being a person that makes a person's choices *theirs*.

## What it actually costs

The cost isn't stupidity. It's something closer to atrophy of nerve. Independent judgment isn't a fact you can look up — it's a muscle built by sitting with discomfort, ambiguity, and the risk of being wrong, over and over, until you trust your own read on things. Early research on this is already surfacing: a 2025 MIT Media Lab study found that people who relied on LLMs to write essays showed measurably reduced neural engagement and weaker memory of their own work compared to those who wrote unaided — and struggled more when the assistance was later taken away. That's not a story about output quality. It's a story about what happens to the muscle when you stop using it.

Scale that up to a generation that has never had to sit with a hard problem unaided, and you don't get a smarter society. You get a more efficient one, populated by people who are individually less equipped to think for themselves — and that asymmetry doesn't distribute its benefits evenly. Someone is still doing the hard thinking. Increasingly, it's a small number of labs deciding, by default, what "good judgment" outputs look like for everyone else.

## The honest counterargument

None of this means offloading is inherently corrosive. Nobody mourns long division. Freeing up cognitive capacity from rote tasks is, on balance, how civilization has always advanced — it's why a modern accountant can reason about tax strategy instead of spending their life adding columns by hand. If AI simply absorbed our trivia and our arithmetic, this would be a non-story.

But judgment isn't arithmetic. Arithmetic has a right answer that exists independent of you. Judgment doesn't — it's constituted by the person making it. When you offload a calculation, you lose nothing of yourself. When you offload a judgment, you lose a little bit of the thing that was making the call. Do that enough times, across enough decisions, and it's fair to ask what's left of the "you" that used to do it.

## The question worth sitting with

Socrates was wrong about memory. He may simply have been early about judgment.

The danger was never that AI would think for us. It's that we'd stop noticing we'd let it — that the outsourcing would feel so convenient, so reasonable, one small email at a time, that we'd never register the moment we stopped trusting ourselves. The uncomfortable question isn't whether AI is capable of good judgment. It's how much of our own we're willing to trade for the relief of not having to use it.
